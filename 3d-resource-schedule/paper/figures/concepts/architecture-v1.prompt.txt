Use case: infographic-diagram.
Create ONE exceptionally polished scientific architecture concept drawing for a DAC computer architecture research paper. This is a VISUAL DESIGN SKETCH for a real 3D compute-in-memory MoE mapping and scheduling framework. Landscape artboard, roughly 3:2, high resolution. Pure white background. Restrained, precise editorial engineering illustration with aligned modules and highly legible short English labels. Navy #254E70, medium blue #2C7FB8, pale blue #DDEAF3, charcoal #202B33; a very small muted amber accent can identify the newly replicated expert. Use crisp fine vector-like outlines, white space, and deliberate typographic hierarchy. The reader must immediately understand both genuinely volumetric 3D stacking and the transformations in the software. Aim for an excellent ACM/IEEE paper figure, beautifully composed, not a marketing poster.

COMPOSITION: two horizontal panels separated by a fine gray rule. Upper panel about 40% height labeled "(a) Hardware Abstraction". Lower panel about 60% height labeled "(b) Mapping and Scheduling Framework". No large overall title.

UPPER PANEL — Make the 3D physical shape unmistakable.
Left: a clean 4 x 4 two-dimensional mesh on a low isometric base. Each mesh site has an upright compact solid multilayer CIM cuboid attached to a router. The links lie in a single plane. Highlight ONE node with a blue outline; use two fine zoom guides toward a larger node in the center. Label simply "2D Mesh".
Center: a prominent accurately drawn isometric STACKED CIM NODE with clear top face, tall front face, and right side face. Draw 8–10 tightly registered horizontal storage layers forming ONE continuous, solid three-dimensional cuboid. Layer boundaries must remain visible on BOTH side faces. The top face has an aligned matrix grid of tiles, and selected weight blocks extend volumetrically inside the stack. Give the volume a cutaway or lightly translucent corner showing actual occupied 3D subvolumes. Draw dimensional arrows H and W along the two base directions and D as a tall vertical arrow. It must look like a stacked three-dimensional volume, with substantial vertical extent, not a fan of loose parallelograms. Under the node, a slim attached logic base contains small, orderly functional strips "Input", "Compute / Reduce", "Output". Add a compact router to one side, and label "Shared Compute Port". Label the whole enlargement "Stacked CIM Node".
Right: two small isometric computation insets sharing the same visual vocabulary. "Planar" highlights ONE horizontal plane within a true layered cube and shows readout to "ADC" then digital "+". "Vertical" highlights an aligned vertical group across multiple layers in a true cube and shows depth accumulation "Σ" then "ADC". Make the distinction visually obvious. A short neat line of interface text underneath reads "Geometry (H, W, D) · Service Time · I/O Traffic".

LOWER PANEL — Give most of the design attention to a readable software architecture. Tell a left-to-right story with meaningful small drawings and a small number of clean directional arrows, instead of a grid of textual boxes.
Arrange FOUR primary regions with aligned English headings:
1. "Inputs & Compilation" (about 19% of width): small MoE layer/expert graph and token–expert route matrix, plus a compact hardware-profile icon. A short compilation arrow transforms these into a set of isometric weight cuboids of different shapes. Use short labels "Model", "Routing Traces", "Hardware", and "Weight-Cubes". The geometry-and-cost representation should visibly connect to the next region.
2. "Offline Weight Mapping" (about 37%): the largest region, a pale blue restrained backing with two clearly separated parallel horizontal tracks labeled "Compute Balanced" and "State-Aware Hypergraph". Use small 4 x 4 node occupancy maps: the first has evenly distributed expert blocks, the second groups related blue expert blocks. Show each track advancing through a small number of budget steps with aligned arrows and the labels "b0", "b1", "bk". Along the advancement, graphically duplicate one amber expert block onto a second node, then show a blue relocation arrow. Short operation labels "Replicate Hotspots" and "Refine Placement" explain this incremental update. Both completed tracks enter ONE concise "Select" junction based on the performance feedback. Give diagrams sufficient visual breathing room.
3. "Deployment" (about 13%): show the selected 4 x 4 physical node map with assigned three-dimensional expert blocks, as a tangible output object. Labels "Replicas" and "Node + Coordinates". A clean arrow sends this deployment to the scheduler.
4. "Online Scheduling" (about 28%): a current token with active experts E1, E2, E3 and a tiny queue motif feed one "Replica + Root Selection" operation. Underneath, show three chosen compute nodes N1, N4, N5 executing colored expert blocks, then two gathering arrows converge at N5 as the reduction root. A compact aligned three-lane execution timeline makes concurrent execution visible. Use the concise execution label "Multicast → Compute → Reduce", and two short phase annotations "Decode: Critical Path" / "Prefill: Queue Balance". The computation nodes and result collection must be easy to follow.

Along the bottom of the software region, a slim integrated "Performance Analysis" band with tiny schematic critical-path segments and node-load bars feeds back through ONE neatly routed dashed arrow to the offline Select junction. Label it "Latency · Critical Path · Resource Load". These tiny visual motifs are conceptual, without numerical performance claims.

Maintain coherent scale, elegant hierarchy, plentiful local whitespace inside dense regions, readable labels, short non-crossing connectors that attach exactly to their objects, and consistent strokes. The true 3D node is the hardware focal point; the parallel mapping tracks and actual runtime execution are the software focal points. All text is English, with correct spelling. Avoid decorative icons, circuit-board wallpaper, glossy renders, heavy shadows, giant arrows, long paragraphs in boxes, and unnecessary embellishment.
